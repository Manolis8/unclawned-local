The Steve Jobs analogy you reached for is right but incomplete. Let me make it
exact.

Jobs said: "The computer is the most remarkable tool we've ever built. It's
like a bicycle for the mind." The GUI made that real — anyone could now see
and touch their ideas. No code required. That unlocked creativity at scale.

Your equivalent sentence should be:
"Agents are the most remarkable staff you've ever had. We make them real —
anyone can now have a team that works for them. No company required."

But that's still not sharp enough. Here's the thing people would say they
can't live without:

  "I don't think about [that task] anymore. It just happens."

Not "I told an AI to do it." Not "I automated it." But the feeling that a
capable person is handling it, knows your preferences, doesn't need to be told
twice, and gets better over time. That is the aha moment. That is delegation.

The real comparison isn't to ChatGPT or Zapier or AutoGPT. The real
comparison is to hiring your first employee. The first time a founder hires
someone who just handles things — the relief, the freed mental space, the
compounding value as they learn the job. That feeling. At a price anyone can
afford. For every person, not just companies.

You are not building a browser automation tool.
You are building the infrastructure for personal delegation.

The product that lets people say: "I have staff."

  PART 2: THE AHA MOMENT — WHAT IT FEELS LIKE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

There are multiple tiers of aha. You need to hit them in order.

  Tier 1 — "It actually works." (Week 1-2)
  The agent does the thing. Correct. First try. User didn't have to babysit it.
  Most tools fail here. You're past this. Good.

  Tier 2 — "It knows me." (Month 1-2)
  The agent posts in YOUR tone, not generic AI voice.
  It knows you prefer bullet points.
  It knows you don't want to follow people back unless they're investors.
  It knows your LinkedIn is professional and your Twitter is casual.
  This is where users stop feeling like they're using a tool and start feeling
  like they have someone who knows them.

  Tier 3 — "I forgot to worry about it." (Month 2-4)
  The agent handles your recurring tasks without you triggering them.
  Monday morning LinkedIn post — already done.
  Price check on a competitor — already in your inbox.
  New followers that match your ICP — already followed back.
  This is the moment that creates the "I can't live without this" response.
  Not because of a feature. Because of what disappears from your mental load.

  Tier 4 — "My agents are better than me at this." (Month 4-12)
  The agent has seen 200 of your tasks. It knows what you actually want vs.
  what you ask for. It surfaces things you didn't think to check.
  It gets better faster than a human employee would.
  This is the moment where it becomes genuinely irreplaceable.

  Tier 5 — "I built this for myself and others want it." (Year 1+)
  The skills marketplace. Other people use the agents you built.
  You make money from what you taught an agent to do.
  The person who trained a perfect "LinkedIn lead gen agent" can sell it.
  The person who built a "Shopify inventory manager" can publish it.
  This is where the Steve Jobs "base for creativity" moment arrives — not
  because you built everything, but because your platform let THEM build it.

  PART 3: WHAT THE BASE ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Steve Jobs built the Mac. It didn't do your work for you. It gave you a
canvas and a set of tools so sharp and natural that your creativity could flow
without friction. The tool disappeared. The work appeared.

Your base needs to feel like that. Here's what it must have before anything
else:

  1. AN AGENT THAT FEELS LIKE A PERSON, NOT A SCRIPT
     Not "step 1: navigate to X, step 2: click Y." The user just says what
     they want and the agent figures it out. When it doesn't know, it asks
     ONE question — not five. When it's about to do something irreversible,
     it shows the user exactly what it will do. This builds trust. Trust is
     the product.

  2. MEMORY THAT COMPOUNDS
     Every task teaches the agent something. Not just skills (how to do a
     thing) but preferences (how YOU want it done). Over time, the agent
     develops a model of the user that makes it faster and more accurate.
     The longer you use it, the less you explain. That compounding is the
     moat. No one will switch because they'd lose their agent's knowledge
     of them.

  3. SKILLS THAT GROW WITH THE USER
     A skill isn't a macro. It's a pattern the agent learned by watching you
     or being trained once. Skills compose — a "research" skill and a
     "write post" skill combine into "research and post." Users don't build
     workflows. They teach the agent once and it generalizes.

  4. THE TEAM LAYER
     One agent is an employee. A team of agents is a department. The user
     should be able to say "I want a research agent, a writing agent, and a
     posting agent that work together." Boss-worker pattern: one agent breaks
     the goal, assigns subtasks, collects results. Shared memory: what one
     agent learns, all agents know. This is what makes complex goals
     achievable — not one superhuman agent, but coordinated specialization.

  5. THE MARKETPLACE
     When a user's agent is great at something, they should be able to
     publish it. Others download and use it. The original creator sees usage
     stats. Eventually: revenue share. This turns your best users into your
     best product developers. The ecosystem compounds the product.

  PART 4: WHAT TO BUILD AND IN WHAT ORDER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The features you listed are mostly right. The order is what matters.
Here's the honest sequence, ruthlessly prioritized:


  ── NOW TO 2 WEEKS ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Priority: Make it undeniably great for ONE person — you.

  The test: Can you go 7 days using your own product daily without being
  frustrated by it? Not "it kind of works." Actually great.

  What that requires:
  - The post flow must be seamless (you've been working on this — good)
  - The permission system must feel natural, not annoying
  - 5-10 skills that work reliably for YOUR actual daily tasks
  - Scheduler that runs at least one daily task without you touching it
  - Memory that actually affects agent behavior (tone, preferences)

  The one question to answer: "What task do I want automated the most?"
  Build that first. Use it every day. Fix what breaks. Ship nothing else.

  The "lovable" integration and other quick wins: yes, some of these CAN
  be fast. A skill that says "create me a landing page" → opens Lovable →
  fills in the prompt → user sees the result in real time. That's actually
  a great demo moment and not hard to build. Could be week 2 material.
  But only AFTER your core daily tasks work reliably.


  ── WEEKS 3-4 (BEFORE TWENTE) ──────────────────────────────────────────────

  Priority: Get the personal AI learning layer working.

  This is Tier 2 of the aha moment — "it knows me." Without this, your
  product is a smart macro runner. With this, it becomes a relationship.

  What to build:
  - After each task completion, a lightweight "what worked, what didn't"
    log that the agent reads at the start of the next similar task
  - Preference extraction: "I noticed you always want bullet points. Always
    apply that?" — simple but powerful
  - Tone learning: when user edits the agent's drafted content, store the
    delta. Learn that this user prefers shorter sentences, no emojis, etc.
  - Context carry-across sessions: agent knows your name, your job, your
    main goals — pulled from a simple user profile you fill in once

  This is also the window to define your user profile system clearly. Not
  just memory of tasks — memory of the USER. Who they are, what they're
  trying to achieve in life, what they've delegated. This context makes
  every future interaction better.


  ── MONTH 1-2 (EARLY TWENTE) ────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Priority: Multi-agent foundation + the first version of the marketplace.

  By now you should be using the product daily and it should feel genuinely
  great. Now you're ready to build for others.

  Multi-agent (supervisor + worker pattern):
  - Define the "team" concept: a named group of agents with roles
  - Boss agent: receives a complex goal, breaks it into subtasks,
    assigns them to specialized worker agents in the team, collects results
  - Shared memory store: one DB namespace per team, all agents can read/write
  - The UI for this doesn't need to be a web app yet — even CLI works:
    "create team: research + write + post" and the boss coordinates

  Skill marketplace (v0.1):
  - Export a skill to a shareable format (JSON + metadata)
  - A simple GitHub-style repo or even a webpage where skills are listed
  - Users can import a skill URL into their instance
  - No payments yet — just community. This creates the ecosystem early.
  - Ratings, usage counts, the "most downloaded" feeling builds culture

  Communities:
  - Start with Discord or a simple forum, not a built-in feature
  - "Investing agents", "coding agents", "content creation agents" — just
    channels where people share their skills and discuss
  - This is cheap to start and tells you what to build based on what people
    actually share and ask for


  ── MONTH 2-4 ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Priority: Persistence, cross-session context, the "I forgot about it" moment.

  - Long-running tasks: agent works over hours/days, checks in when needed
  - Event-driven triggers: "when X happens → agent does Y"
    Email arrives tagged urgent → task created → calendar blocked
    This is the moment where the product stops being reactive (user asks)
    and becomes proactive (agent acts). This is the real step-change.
  - Auto-improving agents: after N runs, a meta-agent reviews the logs and
    suggests or applies improvements. Users see "your agent improved" like
    a fitness tracker showing progress.
  - Desktop app foundation: file access, voice input/output — these open
    up a whole class of tasks that browser-only can't touch

  The auto-improving agent loop is critical and underrated. This is what
  makes the moat DEEPENING over time, not just wide. The longer someone
  uses it, the better their specific agents get — in a way that's
  personalized to them and can't be replicated by someone starting fresh.


  ── MONTH 4-6 ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Priority: Marketplace monetization + the SaaS layer.

  - Creators publish agents/skills with a price tag
  - Revenue share: creator gets 70%, platform gets 30%
  - Users subscribe to agent "packages" (e.g., "Content Creator Pack":
    research + write + schedule + post, $19/month)
  - Verification system: "verified agents" that have been tested and
    perform reliably — this is quality control that makes you trustworthy
  - Agent analytics: creators see how their agents are being used, where
    they fail, what users edit — feedback loop for improvement


  ── YEAR 1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Priority: The platform is the product. You're not building features.
  You're running an ecosystem.

  By year 1, if the above works, you have:
  - Thousands of users with personal agent teams they'd never rebuild from
    scratch (the compounding memory moat)
  - A marketplace with hundreds of agents built by your community
  - Revenue from subscriptions + marketplace cut
  - Data about what humans delegate most, what they trust agents with, what
    they want to control — the most valuable dataset in the AI agent space

  The features that matter at year 1 are no longer about capability.
  They're about trust, reliability, and discovery. Making it easier to
  find the right agent. Making it easier to trust what it does. Making it
  easier to build and share.

  The chat-style templates you listed (Research & Synthesize, Problem Solver,
  Idea Validator, etc.) — these become agent templates in the marketplace.
  Not features you build. Things the community builds and you curate.
  Your job at year 1 is curation and quality, not creation.

  PART 5: THE COFOUNDER AND TWENTE QUESTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Here is the honest answer with no softening.

Don't raise pre-seed right now. The reason is simple: you said the core
value isn't there yet. Raising before you've found the core is raising on
your story and energy, not your product. You'll get bad terms, pressure to
grow metrics that don't matter yet, and a board conversation when you
need silence to think. The Jobs instinct you have — don't ship until it's
great — is incompatible with investor pressure in the early stages.

The one exception: if you find an investor who genuinely gets product
first and will leave you alone for 6 months, and the check is enough to
buy you 18 months of runway without taking a salary. That's rare. Most
pre-seed is not that.

On the cofounder:

The reason to get a cofounder is NOT speed. Speed is a symptom of the
right partnership. The reason to get a cofounder is that the right person
makes the product better in ways you can't see alone. They challenge your
assumptions. They care as much as you do. They're willing to work on this
for 10 years, not 10 months.

Twente is actually the right place. Here's why: CS students who build
things for fun, who stay late, who have side projects — these are people
who already proved they work when no one is paying them. That's the filter.

What to do in the group chat right now:
Write one message. Something like: "Anyone building something on the side
or want to? I've been working on something in the AI agents space for a
while. Looking for people who are serious about building, not just
interested in the idea." Short. No pitch deck. No hype. See who responds
with energy and specifics, not enthusiasm and vagueness.

When you get to Twente:
Give yourself 4-6 weeks before committing to anyone. Work alongside people
before deciding. A cofounder is a 10-year decision. The urgency you feel
right now is real but it will mislead you into accepting the first
interested person rather than the right person.

The cofounder you want is someone who:
- Has already built something (doesn't matter what)
- Finds the engineering problems interesting, not just the vision
- Pushes back on your ideas in a way that makes them better
- Would build this even if no one was watching

What you don't want:
- Someone excited by AI because it's exciting
- Someone who wants to work on YOUR idea but has no ideas of their own
- Someone who needs convincing — the right person will already feel the pull

  PART 6: THE HONEST DIAGNOSIS OF WHERE YOU ARE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

You've built more than most people would have in this time. The permission
system, the skill library, the scheduler, the conversational state — these
are real, working pieces of a real product. That's not nothing.

The core value that's missing is this: the product doesn't yet feel
like YOURS. You built it for yourself and it still feels like a dev tool,
not a personal staff member. The moment it feels like YOUR agent who knows
YOU and handles things without you thinking about it — that's when the
core value arrives.

The gap between where you are and that moment is probably not as wide as
it feels. Here's what the gap actually is:

  1. Memory that actually changes behavior (not just stores facts)
     You need to be able to say "the agent knows I prefer X" and it just
     does X without being told. This requires the preference learning layer.

  2. One or two recurring tasks that run automatically and are actually useful
     The scheduler exists. But does it run anything you actually care about?
     Pick your top 2 daily tasks. Get those running automatically. Once they
     run without you thinking about them, you'll have felt Tier 3 personally.
     Then you'll know what to build for others.

  3. The tone and voice layer
     Your agents speak in generic AI voice. They should speak in YOUR voice.
     Or in the voice you want when communicating professionally. This matters
     more than almost any feature because it's what makes the output feel like
     yours, not like you used a bot.

Get those three things right and you will have your core value. Not as a
roadmap item. As something you feel every morning.

Then ship it to 10 people. Not publicly. 10 people you trust to give you
honest feedback. Watch them. Don't explain anything. See where they get
confused or frustrated. Fix those things. Then 100 people. Then the world.

The Jobs standard isn't about having zero bugs. It's about the core
feeling being undeniably right before you invite people in. You're close.
You know what needs to happen. Now do those three things.

  - Tone/preference learning (watch what user edits, store the delta)
  - User profile (name, job, goals, platforms they use — filled once)
  - 2 automatic daily tasks running on scheduler
  - Lovable / quick-create integration (high demo value, fast to build)
  - Conversational memory that actually changes next-session behavior

  HIGH IMPACT, MEDIUM EFFORT — DO NEXT
  ──────────────────────────────────────
  - Multi-agent team (boss + workers, shared memory)
  - Auto-improving agents (log what worked, meta-agent reads and adjusts)
  - Event-driven triggers (when X → agent does Y)
  - Skill marketplace v0.1 (export/import skills, simple public listing)
  - Desktop app with file access

  HIGH IMPACT, HIGH EFFORT — PLAN FOR LATER
  ───────────────────────────────────────────
  - Full marketplace with payments and revenue share
  - Voice input/output
  - Long-running autonomous tasks (days-long agent work)
  - Agent analytics for creators
  - Enterprise/team accounts

  FEATURES TO DEPRIORITIZE OR SKIP
  ──────────────────────────────────
  - Built-in communities (use Discord first, build it in-app only when you
    have enough users that Discord feels too scattered)
  - The chat templates list (Research & Synthesize, Idea Validator, etc.)
    — these are marketplace items, not core features. Let users build them.
  - Any feature that helps you distribute before the core value is right —
    SEO, referral programs, viral loops — none of that matters yet

The moment that sentence describes what someone actually experiences when
