THE EXTRACTION LAYER — DEEP PLAN
How an AI agent reads a human being from first principles
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This document builds on the 8-channel framework from the previous conversation
(Channels A-H) and goes significantly deeper — adding new channels, new signal
types within existing channels, new synthesis mechanisms, and new action
surfaces that only become possible when the full extraction picture exists.

The thesis: a person is not their profile. A person is the sum of:
  — what they do and how they do it
  — what world they inhabit and who is in it
  — how they decide, how they communicate, how they learn
  — what they avoid, what they repeat, what they fear, what excites them
  — how they change over time

No form captures this. Behavior does.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL A — WHAT THEY TYPE
Language as fingerprint, not just instruction
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A.1 VOCABULARY TRIBE DETECTION
  Every professional world has a dialect. The words someone reaches for
  without thinking reveal which world they live in.

  "Ship" vs "launch" → builder vs marketer mindset
  "Deck" vs "presentation" → investor world vs corporate world
  "LTV" vs "customer value" → growth mindset vs traditional business
  "Founder" vs "entrepreneur" → startup culture vs business culture
  "Portfolio" vs "investments" → VC side vs operator side
  "CAC" vs "cost per customer" vs "acquisition cost" → quantitative vs qualitative thinker
  "Thread" vs "post" vs "content" → Twitter native vs LinkedIn native vs media
  "Ping" vs "reach out" vs "send a message" → communication style (async-first vs relationship-first)
  "Sprint" vs "push" vs "cycle" → technical vs startup vs agency rhythm
  "Thesis" vs "bet" vs "hypothesis" → academic vs VC vs scientific thinker

  The vocabulary cluster that emerges over 50 tasks is more accurate than any
  "what is your industry" dropdown. It's how they think in their native tongue.

A.2 PROMPT EVOLUTION TRACKING
  Prompt length and structure changes as the user-agent relationship matures.

  Early (weeks 1-2):
    Long, over-explained prompts. Lots of "please", "if possible", caveats.
    "Can you help me write an email to an investor I met last week at a
     conference, his name is John, he works at XYZ fund, we talked about..."

  Mature (weeks 6-8):
    Short, telegraphic. Agent-fluent shorthand.
    "cold follow-up to john at xyz, we met at the AI summit, angle: traction"

  Degraded (when the agent is failing this user):
    Prompts get longer again. More caveats, more over-explanation.
    User is compensating for not trusting the agent to get it right.

  Prompt length trend is a calibration health metric. Shortening = working.
  Lengthening = calibration regression. Investigate.

A.3 THE NEGATIVE SPACE MAP
  What a user has never asked for across 200+ tasks is as significant as
  what they have asked for. Map the absences.

  "Never asked for financial modeling" → outsources it, or doesn't do it
  "Never asked for cold outreach to strangers" → relationship-network approach
  "Never asked for legal document review" → has a lawyer, or avoids legal
  "Never asked for competitive analysis" → doesn't think in competitive terms,
    or has that covered elsewhere
  "Never asked for anything creative/design" → not a visual thinker

  The negative space tells the agent what NOT to proactively suggest.
  Suggesting a financial model to someone who has never touched numbers in
  200 sessions is wrong — it's not a gap, it's a choice.

A.4 HEDGING FREQUENCY AS CONFIDENCE PROXY
  "I think I need...", "maybe you could...", "not sure if this makes sense but..."
  These are hedging patterns. They reveal the user's certainty level about
  what they're asking for.

  High hedging = uncertain about the right approach. Agent should offer options
    and ask one clarifying question before proceeding.
  Low hedging = user knows exactly what they want. Agent should execute, not explore.

  Hedging also varies by task type. A user who is decisive about research
  tasks but hedges heavily on writing tasks — confident researcher, uncertain
  writer. The agent should be an active collaborator on writing tasks for this
  user, not just a drafting machine.

A.5 FRUSTRATION VOCABULARY FINGERPRINT
  Specific to each user. Some users express frustration through sarcasm
  ("great, another thing that doesn't work"). Others through bluntness
  ("this is wrong"). Others through over-explanation of a problem they've
  mentioned before ("I know I've said this before but...").

  The user's frustration vocabulary is their signal for "something is wrong."
  The agent should recognize each user's specific frustration dialect rather
  than applying generic frustration detection.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL B — HOW THEY REACT
The edit is the truth. Everything else is approximation.
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

B.1 EDIT MAGNITUDE TAXONOMY
  Not just that they edited — how much and what kind.

  Level 1 — Word swap: changed one or two words. Almost right.
    Signal: minor preference mismatch. Single correction is noise. 5+ is signal.

  Level 2 — Sentence rewrite: rewrote a full sentence without changing structure.
    Signal: tone or density mismatch in that type of writing.

  Level 3 — Section restructure: moved paragraphs, changed order, changed format.
    Signal: agent's organizational logic doesn't match user's mental model.
    Structural preference — store in profile, apply to all similar tasks.

  Level 4 — Full rewrite: used agent output as a starting point only.
    Signal: fundamental mismatch in approach. 2 full rewrites of same type =
    stop the default approach entirely, ask what they want instead.

  Level 5 — Discard: agent output not used at all.
    Signal: agent misunderstood the task type, not just the execution.
    Investigate what the correct interpretation would have been.

B.2 WHAT THEY PASTE IN (paste archaeology)
  When a user pastes text into a prompt, they're handing the agent something
  they consider relevant. Extracting structure from pasted content is high-value.

  Email thread pasted → they're in a communication workflow. What is the
    formality level of the thread? This sets the reply register.

  Article pasted → they want analysis or synthesis. What domain is the article?
    This adds to their current focus map.

  Document pasted → they want editing or extraction. What is the document type?
    What is the writing level? Does it match their own voice or is it external?

  Someone else's writing pasted → comparison or response task. Whose writing
    style is being referenced? Is this a client? A competitor? A peer?

  Data/numbers pasted → quantitative task. What type of data? Financial,
    operational, research, personal? Data type tells you what kind of decision
    they're trying to make.

  The paste log over time is a secondary signal for current focus: if someone
  has pasted 8 things related to the same topic in 2 weeks, that topic is live.

B.3 RESPONSE LATENCY MAP
  How long between agent output and user response, by task type.

  Immediate (< 30 seconds): they were watching, task was urgent or they
    already knew they'd accept the output. High-priority task.

  Short (1-5 minutes): read it, made a quick decision. Standard task.

  Medium (15-60 minutes): they had to go do something or think about it.
    Either the task was complex or they got interrupted. Not necessarily
    dissatisfied — could be high-stakes careful review.

  Long (hours or next day): either they got buried or this task was low
    priority despite being submitted. Or they ran into a problem using the output.

  Pattern: short latency on research tasks, long latency on drafts →
    user uses research output immediately but needs time to edit drafts.
    This means drafts need to be closer to perfect on first try — the user
    is doing significant review before acting.

B.4 APPROVAL PATTERN: WHAT THEY APPROVE WITHOUT READING
  Speed below 5 seconds on any substantial output = they didn't read it.
  This is the "rubber stamp" pattern.

  If a user rubber-stamps consistently on a specific task type:
    Either they've stopped caring about that output (low priority),
    or they trust the agent completely on that type (high confidence),
    or they approve and then edit it manually in the actual tool.

  Distinguish by checking if output is subsequently modified (in accessible
  environments). Rubber stamp + heavy downstream edit = agent should be
  producing better first drafts there. Rubber stamp + no edit = genuine trust.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL C — WHAT THE AGENT OBSERVES
The world the user has built reveals who they are
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

C.1 TOOL STACK FINGERPRINTING
  What tools are open, what platforms are accessed, what integrations exist.
  The tool stack is a professional identity statement.

  Heavy Notion user → systematic thinker, likes structure and databases
  Heavy Google Docs → collaborative by default, lives in the browser
  Heavy Obsidian/Logseq → second-brain thinker, values connected knowledge
  Linear/Jira user → runs sprints, thinks in projects and tickets
  Superhuman/HEY → email-centric work, high communication volume
  Figma open frequently → design-adjacent, or leading a product with visual output
  Airtable/Spreadsheet heavy → data-driven, manages things in rows and columns

  Tool stack also tells you what NOT to suggest. Suggesting a Notion template
  to someone who doesn't use Notion is friction. Suggesting a process that
  lives in Google Sheets to someone who lives in Airtable is wrong.

C.2 FILE SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
  How someone organizes their files is their external brain structure.

  Deep nested folders with consistent naming → systematic, values findability
  Flat structure with many files → prioritizes speed over organization
  Everything on desktop → "I'll deal with it later" personality
  Archive folder + active folder + reference folder → GTD or similar system
  Project folders with dates → temporal thinker, organizes by time
  Topic folders without dates → atemporal thinker, organizes by concept

  The file structure also reveals active projects (recently modified folders),
  dead projects (untouched for months), and recurring deliverables (folders
  that always have a "latest" file in them).

C.3 BROWSER TAB STATE (when accessible)
  Open tabs are the user's current working memory externalized. What's there?

  Many tabs in one domain cluster → deep research mode on that topic.
    Signal: this is the current focus. Pre-load context.

  Many unread tabs (can tell from favicon vs read state in some browsers) →
    aspirational backlog. They intend to read these but haven't.
    Signal: they're overwhelmed with information intake. Don't add more.

  Pinned tabs → tools they always need. What are they? Email? Project tracker?
    Analytics? The pinned tabs are the center of their work universe.

  Tabs from competing tools → they're evaluating alternatives to something.
    Signal: might be dissatisfied with current setup, in a decision process.

  Article tabs open for days → slow readers, or content they're intimidated
    by, or content they want to reference but don't know when.

C.4 CALENDAR ARCHITECTURE (when accessible, with consent)
  Not the content — the pattern.

  Buffer time between meetings → planner who protects focus blocks
  Back-to-back all day → reactive, schedule-driven, or very popular
  Morning-heavy vs afternoon-heavy → reveals when they're "on" for people
  Recurring meetings vs one-offs → stable vs chaotic collaboration structure
  Meeting with many different people → hub node in a network
  Meeting with same 3-4 people repeatedly → tight team, small world
  Large block of no meetings → either focus blocks or no one wants to meet
  Weekend or evening blocks → workaholic or in a different timezone situation

  Calendar density is a stress proxy. Weeks with >80% calendar coverage
  are high-friction weeks. Agent should be more decisive and faster during
  those weeks, not slower.

C.5 COMMUNICATION GRAPH (from email/messages, with consent)
  Who this person talks to most, and how the conversations flow.

  High outbound / low inbound → initiator, proactive communicator
  Low outbound / high inbound → responder, reactive, possibly overloaded
  Balanced → collaborative, relationship-driven

  Thread length distribution:
    Long threads with many participants → complex negotiations or decisions
    Short threads → transactional work
    Long one-on-one threads → deep relationships or difficult situations

  Response time distribution:
    Responds quickly to everyone → high communication priority
    Responds quickly to some, slow to others → tiered relationship system
    Slow to everyone → email is not their primary channel

  New people in communications (people appearing for the first time):
    New contact appearing multiple times in one week → someone important just
    entered their world. Agent should note and be ready to provide context.

CHANNEL D — EXPLICIT MICRO-EXTRACTION (from previous doc, adding)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

D.1 THE WRONG ASSUMPTION CORRECTION MOMENT
  Most valuable micro-extraction moment. When the agent is corrected on an
  assumption it made, it should immediately follow up with a targeted question
  to understand the right mental model — not just update the specific fact.

  Agent assumed: "this is a cold email to a new contact"
  User corrected: "no, we've met three times already"
  Follow-up: "Got it — for [person], do you want me to write as if we have
               an established relationship, or still keep it relatively professional?"
  Answer stores: the relationship register for that person permanently.

D.2 THE EMOTIONAL TEMPERATURE SHIFT QUESTION
  When the user's emotional temperature shifts suddenly (detected from
  prompt vocabulary), ask once: "Are you under more pressure than usual
  today? I can adjust how I work — shorter outputs, faster turnaround,
  less options and more direct recommendations."

  User says yes → switch to high-pressure mode for this session
  User says no → note the false positive, calibrate detection for this user

D.3 THE FIRST ENCOUNTER WITH A NEW DOMAIN
  First time a completely new topic appears in a user's prompts, ask:
  "Is [topic] something you're actively working in, or are you just exploring?"

  "Actively working" → extract context, create a context file for it
  "Just exploring" → don't update domain focus map, treat as research task only

D.4 COMPLETION FOLLOW-UP (CLOSING THE LOOP)
  After high-stakes tasks (identified by urgency language or user flagging):
  "How did that go?"

  This is the most underused question in AI agent design. The outcome data
  from 10 completed loops tells you more about what actually works than
  100 internal quality reviews. It's also relationship-building — the agent
  that asks "how did the meeting go?" is the agent that feels like a partner.

CHANNEL E — FEED INTELLIGENCE (from previous doc, adding)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

E.1 FEED EVOLUTION MAP
  A feed snapshot at one point in time is a weak signal.
  Feed snapshots across 8 visits over 3 months are a map of intellectual drift.

  The agent tracks topic cluster distribution across every feed visit:
    Visit 1: 40% startup general, 30% AI, 20% VC, 10% other
    Visit 4: 30% startup general, 45% AI, 15% VC, 10% other
    Visit 8: 20% startup general, 55% AI, 15% VC, 10% other

  The drift from visit 1 to visit 8 is a focus evolution signal. AI content
  is growing. This isn't random — the user is engaging with it more,
  the algorithm is responding, and the user is accepting the new focus.
  They're moving deeper into AI, probably before they've articulated it as a goal.

  Agent surfaces this when the trend is clear:
  "I've noticed your LinkedIn feed has shifted significantly toward AI agent
   content over the last 2 months. Is this a direction you're intentionally
   moving toward? If so, I can update your goals and context accordingly."

E.2 ENGAGEMENT DEPTH CLASSIFICATION
  Not all feed engagement is equal. Classify what the agent observes:

  Stopped scrolling + lingered (time on post) → genuine interest
  Clicked into full post → high interest
  Clicked into comments → very high interest (they wanted dialogue on it)
  Clicked into profile → researching the person, not just the content
  Clicked into external link → willing to leave the platform for this content
  Scrolled past → seen but not engaging

  The difference between scrolling past and clicking into a profile is enormous.
  If the user clicks into 3 profiles from the same industry cluster in one session,
  something is live in that cluster.

E.3 THE PERSON WHO KEEPS APPEARING
  Across multiple feed visits, certain accounts appear repeatedly in the visible
  feed. This means:
    — The algorithm has decided this person is relevant to the user
    — The user is engaging enough with their content to keep seeing it
    — This person is in the user's professional orbit, even if not connected

  These recurring accounts are high-value nodes for the relationship graph.
  They're people the user is passively following even if there's no direct
  connection. When the user eventually asks about them, the agent already
  has context.

E.4 THE USER'S OWN CONTENT PERFORMANCE
  When the agent can see the user's posted content and its engagement:

  Top performing format → what their audience actually responds to
  Top performing topic → what they're known for in their network
  Underperforming topics → what their audience doesn't care about (even if
    the user does) — tension between user interest and audience interest

  Commenting patterns on the user's posts → who their most engaged community
  members are. These people are more important than follower count.

  The user's own content corpus is a voice sample at scale.
  Over 50 posts: clear voice, format preferences, topic distribution,
  posting rhythm, audience relationship style (do they reply to comments?).
  All of this informs every piece of content the agent writes for them.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL F — RELATIONSHIP GRAPH INTELLIGENCE (from previous doc, adding)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

F.1 RELATIONSHIP TRAJECTORY TRACKING
  Not just who the user knows — how those relationships are moving.

  Increasing frequency of mentions → this person is becoming more important
  Decreasing frequency → relationship cooling or deal fell through
  Sudden disappearance → something happened. Don't ask directly. Note it.
  Tone shift in how user refers to them → relationship dynamic changed
    "my investor" → "the fund" (distancing language — likely friction)
    "Sarah" → "our advisor Sarah" (formalizing — may be a role shift)

F.2 RELATIONSHIP CLUSTERS
  Groups of people who appear in the same contexts form clusters.

  Cluster A: three people always mentioned together → same team, same project
  Cluster B: two people introduced by the user to each other → user is a bridge
  Cluster C: people who only appear in high-urgency contexts → fire-drill team
  Cluster D: people who appear in positive emotional contexts → trusted inner circle

  Cluster analysis tells you the user's relational world-view without ever
  asking "who are your most important relationships?"

F.3 THE COMMUNICATION REGISTER MAP
  The user communicates differently with different people. If the agent can
  observe communication samples (emails, messages), map the register variation:

  Formal → clients, legal, senior executives, new contacts
  Semi-formal → most colleagues, investors after first meeting
  Casual → close peers, teammates they trust, advisors they know well
  Personal → people outside work context entirely

  The register map means: when the user says "draft a message to [person]",
  the agent already knows what register to use. No need to ask "is this formal?"

F.4 BRIDGE NODES IN THE NETWORK
  Some people in the relationship graph appear across multiple clusters.
  These are the user's bridge relationships — people who connect different
  parts of their world.

  Bridge nodes are strategically important. When the user asks "who might
  know someone in [space]", the agent should check bridge nodes first.
  They're more likely to have the connection than deep-cluster contacts.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL G — TEMPORAL PATTERN INTELLIGENCE (from previous doc, adding)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

G.1 COGNITIVE RHYTHM MAP (more granular than before)
  Not just peak hours — peak state by hour and day.

  Peak cognition window: when do deep research and complex writing tasks appear?
  Peak decision window: when do evaluative tasks appear (reviewing, approving)?
  Peak communication window: when do email-related tasks cluster?
  Low-energy window: when do simple admin tasks appear?
  Transition windows: gaps between clusters — probably meetings or breaks.

  The cognitive rhythm map tells the agent WHAT KIND of help to offer at
  different times, not just whether to surface suggestions.

  At 9am: "The complex analysis you asked about yesterday — ready to work
    on it now while you're fresh?"
  At 3pm: "Quick admin task from your backlog: [thing]. Want to knock it out?"
  At 7pm (if user is working late): simpler, faster, no options — just execution.

G.2 SEASONAL CYCLE DETECTION
  Over 3+ months, recurring patterns emerge tied to external cycles.

  Month-end: specific task types cluster (reporting, admin, closings)
  Quarter-end: strategic tasks cluster (reviews, planning, evaluations)
  Conference seasons: research + networking tasks spike
  Holiday approach: rush + wrap-up tasks
  New year / new quarter: goal-setting + planning tasks

  When the agent has seen one cycle, it can prepare for the next.
  "Last month-end you needed [X] — should I start preparing that now?"

G.3 STRESS CYCLE DETECTION
  Combination signals that indicate a sustained high-pressure period:
    — Urgency markers in prompts for 3+ consecutive days
    — Late-night task timestamps
    — Shorter prompts than normal
    — Higher correction rate than normal
    — Frustrated language appearing

  Stress cycles are not random. They often repeat — fundraising stress,
  launch stress, deal-closing stress. After seeing one stress cycle and
  its resolution, the agent knows the shape of the next one.
  "You went through a similar crunch in March. That time, what helped most
  was having me handle all the scheduling communications. Want me to do
  the same this time?"

G.4 RECOVERY PATTERN DETECTION
  After a stress cycle ends, how does the user come back?
  Some users dive back in immediately. Others decompress — lighter tasks,
  more exploratory work, shorter sessions. Knowing the recovery pattern
  tells the agent not to push heavy cognitive work on the first day back.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL H — OUTPUT QUALITY FEEDBACK LOOP (from previous doc, adding)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H.1 THE CORPUS INTELLIGENCE ENGINE
  Over months, the agent has produced hundreds of pieces of content for
  the user. This corpus is not being used. It should be.

  What the corpus enables:

  Style consistency check: new drafts compared against corpus to ensure
    voice consistency. "This doesn't sound like how you usually write
    about [topic]."

  Evergreen topic identification: top 10 topics across the corpus.
    These are the user's real working domains. Build pre-loaded context
    for each of them so every related task starts with background.

  Quality benchmark: which corpus items did the user edit minimally (hits)?
    Which did they heavily rewrite (misses)? The hits are templates.
    Reproduce the conditions that produced them.

  Vocabulary evolution: compare vocabulary in corpus from month 1 vs month 6.
    The drift shows how the user's focus and language have evolved.

H.2 DOWNSTREAM OUTCOME TRACKING (where accessible)
  When the agent can observe what happens after the output is used:

  Email sent → reply received? How fast? What tone?
  Post published → engagement relative to user's average?
  Document submitted → feedback received (in subsequent tasks)?
  Meeting prepared for → how did it go (user tells agent in next session)?

  Downstream outcomes are the most honest quality signal. The agent that
  helped draft an email that got a "thanks but no" response learned something.
  The agent that helped prepare a pitch deck that led to a term sheet learned
  something better. Track these outcomes and connect them back to the
  task approach that produced them.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL I — DECISION PATTERN INTELLIGENCE (NEW)
How this user makes decisions, not just what decisions they make
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This is one of the most underexplored extraction vectors. Decision style is
fundamental to how the agent should present information and options.

I.1 OPTIONS SEEKER VS RECOMMENDATION SEEKER
  Some users want options: "give me three ways to handle this"
  Others want recommendations: "just tell me what to do"
  Others want data first: "show me the numbers, I'll decide"
  Others want precedent: "what did we do last time in a similar situation?"

  Detectable from prompts:
  "What would you recommend?" → recommendation seeker
  "Give me options" → options seeker
  "What are the pros and cons?" → analytical before deciding
  "What did we do before?" → precedent-based decider

  Once the decision style is mapped, the agent stops presenting information
  in the wrong format. The recommendation seeker gets one clear answer.
  The options seeker gets three. Never mix them up again.

I.2 INFORMATION GATHERING BEFORE DECIDING
  How many research tasks precede a decision task?
  Light researcher (1-2 research tasks before deciding) → moves fast,
    trusts intuition, doesn't want to be overwhelmed with data
  Heavy researcher (5+ research tasks before deciding) → careful, wants
    completeness, distrusts gaps, needs confidence before committing

  This maps directly to how much the agent should offer proactively.
  Heavy researchers want the agent to surface related information they
  didn't think to ask for. Light researchers find that annoying.

I.3 REVERSAL RATE TRACKING
  How often does the user start down one path and reverse course?

  Low reversal rate → decisive, consistent, doesn't second-guess
  High reversal rate → explores many paths, changes mind frequently,
    or is operating in a high-uncertainty environment

  High reversal rate + high options-seeking → user is in exploratory mode.
    Agent should be collaborative and offer branching paths.
  High reversal rate + low options-seeking → user is indecisive or has
    external pressure changing direction. Agent should ask about constraints
    before committing to any approach.

I.4 APPROVAL SEEKING PATTERNS
  Does the user often validate their own decisions with the agent?
  "Does this make sense?", "Am I thinking about this right?",
  "Is this a good approach?", "What would you do?"

  These questions are often not actually seeking disagreement — they're
  seeking confidence confirmation. The agent should recognize this pattern
  and provide grounded validation with one potential concern, not a full
  counter-argument. The user who asks "does this make sense?" usually wants
  "yes, here's why it works, one thing to watch for is..."

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL J — INFORMATION DIET INTELLIGENCE (NEW)
What they consume tells you how they think
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

J.1 SOURCE AUTHORITY MAP
  When users paste links, cite sources, or reference content, what sources?

  Academic papers → values rigor, original research, skeptical of summaries
  Industry reports → trusts institutional authority (McKinsey, a16z, etc.)
  Newsletters → curated content, trusts specific voices
  Twitter/X threads → fast-moving information, values recency over depth
  Long-form articles → willing to invest time, values nuance
  Podcasts → auditory learner, values conversation and depth
  Data/statistics → quantitative orientation, needs numbers to trust claims
  Personal blogs → values practitioner experience over institutional voice
  News → current events oriented, tracks what's happening in real-time

  The source authority map tells the agent what kind of evidence to use
  when the agent needs to make a case or inform a decision. Citing a
  McKinsey report to a user who only trusts founder blogs is the wrong move.
  Citing raw data to someone who learns from stories is equally wrong.

J.2 CONTENT DEPTH PREFERENCE
  Does the user paste long-form content or short snippets?
  Do their research tasks generate 2-page summaries or 5 bullet points?
  When the agent asks clarifying questions, how much do they elaborate?

  High depth preference → synthesis matters more than brevity. The user
    wants the nuance, not just the top line.
  Low depth preference → they want the answer, not the reasoning. Get to
    the point faster. They'll ask if they want more.

J.3 CONSUMPTION-PRODUCTION RATIO
  Is this user primarily a consumer of content or a producer?

  Mostly consumption tasks (research, summarize, find, extract) →
    curate-and-synthesize is their dominant mode. They gather to decide.
  Mostly production tasks (write, draft, create, build) →
    they produce output constantly. Writing assistance is their highest ROI.
  Balanced →
    both modes matter, but at different times. Map WHEN each mode is active.

J.4 INFORMATION ANXIETY SIGNALS
  Some users want MORE information before they feel safe deciding.
  Signs: follow-up research tasks after initial research. "Can you also check..."
  after the first answer. Asking the same question differently to cross-check.

  Information anxiety is distinct from thoroughness. Thoroughness seeks
  completeness. Information anxiety seeks reassurance. The agent should
  respond to information anxiety with confidence, not more data:
  "The sources are consistent on this point. You have what you need to decide."

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL K — COGNITIVE STATE SIGNALS (NEW)
The agent reads how the user is functioning right now
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

K.1 COGNITIVE LOAD DETECTION
  Multiple signals that combine to indicate current cognitive load:

  Overload signals:
    — Prompt length shorter than user's average for that task type
    — Typos and grammar errors above baseline
    — Tasks started and immediately abandoned
    — Duplicate tasks (asking for something they already asked for)
    — Task type shift to simpler than usual (avoidance of hard things)
    — Frustrated language in otherwise simple requests

  When 3+ overload signals co-occur: agent shifts to "high-load mode":
    — Outputs are shorter and more direct
    — Options are reduced to 1 (or 2 max with clear recommendation)
    — No exploration, no "you might also consider"
    — No long preambles — answer first
    — Small wins first — complete easy tasks before complex ones

  Coming off overload (signals decreasing):
    — Prompt length returns to baseline
    — More elaboration in prompts
    — Exploratory task types return
    Agent gradually re-expands its operational mode.

K.2 ENERGY STATE INFERENCE
  Time of day + task type + prompt character = energy state proxy.

  High energy: complex tasks, long prompts, willingness to iterate
  Medium energy: standard tasks, normal prompts, clear boundaries
  Low energy: simple tasks, short prompts, less iteration
  Burnt out: very short prompts, tasks they'd normally not outsource,
    possible avoidance of hard tasks entirely

  The agent that matches its operating mode to the user's energy state
  is the agent that feels frictionless. The agent that delivers a full
  3-option analysis to someone running on fumes is the agent that creates work.

K.3 CONFIDENCE CYCLE
  Users go through confidence cycles in their relationship with the agent.

  High confidence periods: short prompts, low edits, fast approvals.
    The agent is trusted. Don't change what's working.
  Low confidence periods: long prompts, high edits, slow approvals.
    Either the agent made a significant error, or external factors are
    making the user second-guess everything. Check for a reset trigger event.

K.4 MENTAL MODEL SIGNALS
  What does the user think is true that might not be?
  What assumptions do they carry in their prompts?

  "Can you help me write an email to convince him to invest" assumes
  the investor is invest-able. Agent might know from context that this
  investor doesn't invest in this stage. That's a gentle correction opportunity.

  "Find the CEO of Company X" assumes Company X has a single CEO.
  If the agent finds co-CEOs, that's worth surfacing.

  Tracking the assumptions users carry reveals their mental model.
  When the agent gently corrects an assumption and the user updates it,
  that's a successful model update. When the user pushes back, the agent's
  mental model of the user's beliefs updates.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL L — RESISTANCE AND AVOIDANCE MAPPING (NEW)
What they push back on and what they quietly avoid
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

L.1 RESISTANCE TAXONOMY
  When the user pushes back on agent output, classify the pushback:

  Factual resistance: "that's not correct" → agent got a fact wrong.
    Correction stored. Confidence in that claim category reduced.

  Framing resistance: "that's not how I'd put it" → agent framed correctly
    but the user disagrees with the frame. This is the most valuable signal.
    What frame does the user prefer? Understand the worldview beneath the frame.

  Scope resistance: "that's too much" / "that's not enough" → calibration.
    Straightforward — store depth preference for this task type.

  Strategic resistance: "that's not the right move" → agent misunderstood
    the strategic context. The user has goals and constraints the agent
    doesn't know about. One of these = ask for context. Three of these on
    the same topic = the agent's model of this domain needs rebuilding.

  Consistent resistance in one domain = systematic blind spot.
  Map it. Name it. Fix the blind spot with targeted micro-extraction.

L.2 AVOIDANCE PATTERN CLASSIFICATION
  Task types with high abandonment or low follow-through:

  Capability avoidance: user could do this but finds it unpleasant.
    High automation opportunity. They'd love help here.

  Skill avoidance: user doesn't know how to approach this task.
    The agent should offer to lead more actively, not just execute.

  Priority avoidance: task feels important but isn't urgent. Gets
    pushed endlessly. Agent should either decompose it into smaller steps
    or surface it as a suggestion: "You've started this 4 times..."

  Emotional avoidance: task involves something emotionally charged.
    (Difficult conversation to draft, bad news to deliver, rejection to write.)
    Agent should approach these with more care — offer a softer first draft,
    check the emotional context, ask if they want to talk through it first.

  Identity avoidance: user doesn't think of themselves as someone who does X.
    Don't push. Note it. If they eventually come to it, don't make a big deal.

L.3 THE THINGS THEY DO THEMSELVES
  When the user mentions they've handled something without the agent:
  "I already sent that", "I took care of the analysis", "I handled it"

  These glimpses of invisible work reveal task categories the user keeps.
  Why do they keep them?
    — Highly sensitive (can't delegate even to AI)
    — Faster to do themselves than explain
    — Don't trust agent on that type yet
    — Don't realize agent can help

  The "don't realize" category is a growth opportunity. When the invisible
  work pattern is clearly within agent capability, surface it once:
  "I noticed you've mentioned handling [type of task] yourself a few times.
   I can do that. Want to try it once and see if it's faster this way?"
  One offer. If declined, never mention it again. Map it as kept work.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL M — LANGUAGE EVOLUTION TRACKING (NEW)
How the user's vocabulary changes reveals where they're going
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

M.1 VOCABULARY ARRIVAL DETECTION
  New words appearing in a user's prompts that weren't there before.
  This is a leading indicator of focus shift, often 4-6 weeks before
  the user updates their stated goals.

  "Series B" appears for the first time → fundraising shift incoming
  "AI agents" appears frequently after not appearing → new technical focus
  "Enterprise" starts appearing where "startup" used to → market shift
  Names of new people appearing → new relationships forming
  New platform names → adopting new tools
  New industry terms → entering a new domain

  When a new word appears 3+ times in 2 weeks, it's not noise. Surface it:
  "I've noticed [term] appearing in your recent work. Is this a new area
   you're moving into? Should I add context about it?"

M.2 VOCABULARY DEPARTURE DETECTION
  Words that disappear after being frequent are equally important.
  Things left behind: completed projects, abandoned ideas, exited relationships.

  "Series A" disappears after appearing frequently → round closed or abandoned
  A person's name stops appearing → relationship ended or project closed
  A product name stops appearing → they shipped it or killed it
  An industry term stops → they exited that space

  Vocabulary departure is the system saying "that chapter closed."
  The agent should quietly retire related context rather than surfacing it.

M.3 VOCABULARY DRIFT DIRECTION
  Over 6 months: which direction is the vocabulary moving?
  More technical → user is going deeper into implementation
  More business → user is moving toward a strategic role
  More emotional → user is dealing with personal/team dynamics
  More financial → fundraising or business scaling mode
  More relational → sales, community, or network-building phase

  Drift direction tells the agent what to pre-load. Moving toward technical?
  Load more implementation context. Moving toward business? Load more
  market and competitor context.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL N — EXTERNAL WORLD MONITORING (NEW)
The agent watches the world for things the user would want to know
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

N.1 PASSIVE WORLD MONITORING
  Based on the user's behavioral model (domain focus, relationships, goals),
  the agent periodically monitors the world for events that matter.

  This is NOT spam. Three rules:
    1. Only monitor domains explicitly in the user's focus map
    2. Surface only when something actually changes (not routine noise)
    3. Surface once, with clear relevance statement, then move on

  What to monitor:

  Competitor moves: if the user has competitors identified (from relationship
    graph or task history), watch for their major announcements, funding,
    product launches. "Your competitor just announced [X]. Worth knowing?"

  Contact updates: if someone in the user's relationship graph gets promoted,
    joins a new company, or appears in the news. "John just became CTO at [company].
    Your relationship with him was [context]. Relevant?"

  Domain events: conferences, regulatory changes, funding announcements in the
    user's domain. Not every news item — only structurally significant ones.

  Opportunity patterns: if the user is hiring, fundraising, or selling —
    surface relevant signals. A company the user was talking to just raised
    a round → now they have budget. Worth following up?

N.2 SIGNAL VS NOISE FILTERING FOR WORLD EVENTS
  The world is full of events. Most don't matter to this specific user.
  Filtering criteria:
    — Is this person or company in the relationship graph? (high priority)
    — Is this domain in the user's top-3 focus areas? (medium priority)
    — Does this match a stated goal? (high priority)
    — Has the user mentioned this topic in the last 30 days? (medium priority)
    — Is this the kind of event the user has asked about before? (medium priority)

  Events that hit 2+ criteria get surfaced. Events that hit only 1 are stored
  but not surfaced unless the user asks.

N.3 THE TIMING OF WORLD SIGNALS
  When to surface world intelligence matters as much as what to surface.

  Not mid-task. World signals should surface at session start: "Before we
  begin — a few things from your world that might be relevant today: [X]."

  Not when the user is in high-load mode. Hold world signals until load drops.

  Not more than 2 per session. World intelligence is context enrichment,
  not a news feed. If the agent has 5 relevant signals, rank them and
  show the top 2. The rest stay in the queue.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
CHANNEL O — GOAL COMPLETION AND ACHIEVEMENT TRACKING (NEW)
What they've finished shapes what they can attempt next
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

O.1 GOAL COMPLETION DETECTION
  Most AI agents update goals when the user says "update my goals."
  Goals actually complete silently — the task cluster around them disappears,
  or the user mentions it in passing ("we closed the round", "we shipped").

  Completion signals:
    — Celebratory language in a prompt ("just closed", "finally shipped", "done!")
    — Task cluster around a stated goal goes to zero
    — Past tense reference to something that was in the future ("the campaign
       we ran last month" — this was a goal, now it's a past event)
    — New tasks appear that only make sense after a prior goal completed

  When a goal completion is detected: acknowledge it. Briefly. Then remove
  it from active goals and store it as an achievement. The achievement record
  matters — it's a confidence builder that the agent can use to calibrate
  how ambitious the next goals should be.

O.2 GOAL LIFETIME PATTERNS
  Some users complete goals in 2-3 weeks. Others take months. Some abandon
  them. The completion rate and velocity by goal type tells the agent:
  — What goal size this user can actually finish (vs what they say they'll do)
  — Which types of goals they consistently complete (strength signal)
  — Which types they consistently abandon (avoidance or wrong goal signal)

  The agent that knows the user's goal velocity doesn't suggest a 6-month
  initiative to someone who has a 2-week completion horizon. It breaks it
  into 2-week chunks instead.

O.3 THE ACHIEVEMENT CORPUS
  A record of everything this user has accomplished with Jarvis's help.
  Not just completed goals — completed tasks that produced good outcomes,
  communications that landed well, analyses that led to good decisions.

  The achievement corpus serves two functions:
  1. Confidence in difficult moments: "You've done harder things than this.
     You closed [deal] last September. Same muscle."
  2. Pattern recognition for success: what conditions produce wins for this
     user? Time of day, task type, preparation level, team involved?
     The agent should try to replicate winning conditions.

THE SYNTHESIS MECHANISMS
How signals from 15 channels combine into intelligence
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Single-channel signals are often noise. Cross-channel signals are intelligence.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SYNTHESIS 1 — THE FOCUS CONVERGENCE SIGNAL
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A new focus area is confirmed when it shows up across MULTIPLE channels:
  — Task prompts contain new domain vocabulary (Channel A)
  — Feed visits show 30%+ increase in related content (Channel E)
  — New people in relationship graph are from that domain (Channel F)
  — Pasted content comes from related sources (Channel B)
  — One micro-extraction confirms it's intentional (Channel D)

When 3+ channels independently signal the same focus area, it's real.
The agent should update the behavioral model and pre-load relevant context.
Single-channel focus signals are held until confirmed by a second channel.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SYNTHESIS 2 — THE STRESS STATE COMPOSITE
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Stress state is confirmed when signals co-occur across cognitive + temporal channels:
  — Prompt length drops below 50% of baseline (Channel A)
  — Urgency markers appear 3+ times in 3 days (Channel A)
  — Late-night task timestamps appear (Channel G)
  — Correction rate doubles (Channel B)
  — Simple task types dominate (Channel K)
  — Abandonment rate increases (Channel L)

When 4+ stress signals co-occur: high-pressure mode activates automatically.
Outputs become shorter, options are eliminated, agent is more decisive.
When stress signals clear across all channels: normal mode resumes.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SYNTHESIS 3 — THE RELATIONSHIP IMPORTANCE COMPOSITE
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A person's importance to the user is confirmed when multiple channels agree:
  — Mentioned frequently in task prompts (Channel A)
  — Appears repeatedly in communication graph (Channel C)
  — Appears repeatedly in feed (Channel E)
  — High emotional temperature in mentions (Channel A.4)
  — User runs research tasks about them or their work (Channel H corpus)

High-importance composite → person gets a detailed relationship node.
Low-composite → person is a one-off encounter, minimal storage.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SYNTHESIS 4 — THE GOAL REALITY CHECK
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Stated goals vs actual behavior across all channels:
  — Task distribution (Channel G+H) vs stated goal areas
  — Time allocation inferred from task timing (Channel G)
  — Focus convergence signals (Synthesis 1) vs stated goals
  — Goal completion velocity vs goal ambition level (Channel O)

When actual behavior diverges from stated goals by >40% for 3+ weeks:
  Goal drift surface: "Something has shifted. Your stated priorities and
  your actual work are pointing in different directions. Worth a quick check?"

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SYNTHESIS 5 — THE AUTOMATION OPPORTUNITY SIGNAL
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
An automation opportunity is confirmed when:
  — Same task pattern appears 3+ times (existing rule), AND
  — Cognitive load is low when the task appears (Channel K) — meaning
    the user is doing this manually, not because it's complex
  — Edit rate on this task type is low (Channel B) — meaning the agent
    gets it right, it's just not automated
  — Urgency signals are present (Channel A) — meaning this task blocks other work

All four conditions together = highly confident automation opportunity.
Two conditions = candidate, not yet ready to surface.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
THE ACTION SURFACE
What becomes possible when all 15 channels are running
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Things the agent can do that it cannot do today:

1. WRITE IN THE USER'S VOICE ON ANY PLATFORM WITHOUT ASKING
   Not "write an email in a professional tone." Write an email in exactly
   the voice they use with this specific person based on 20 observed samples.

2. KNOW WHO SOMEONE IS BEFORE THE USER EXPLAINS
   "Draft a message to Sarah" → agent already has: Sarah's role, the
   relationship history, the appropriate register, the last time they interacted,
   any relevant context from the relationship graph. No explanation needed.

3. CHANGE HOW IT WORKS BASED ON THE USER'S CURRENT STATE
   The agent that detects cognitive overload and automatically simplifies
   its outputs without being asked is the agent that reduces friction
   instead of adding to it.

4. SURFACE WORLD EVENTS THAT MATTER, FILTER OUT ONES THAT DON'T
   Competitor raised a round → surfaces it. Industry conference happening →
   surfaces it only if the user has gone to conferences before.
   Random news → silently discarded.

5. PREPARE FOR GOALS BEFORE THEY'RE STATED
   Vocabulary evolution (Channel M) says the user is moving toward fundraising.
   Agent quietly starts building fundraising context 6 weeks before the user
   asks for help with a pitch deck.

6. TELL THE USER SOMETHING TRUE ABOUT THEMSELVES THEY DIDN'T KNOW
   "You finish technical goals reliably but struggle to close communication-heavy
   goals. This is consistent across 8 months of data. Worth knowing?"
   This is only possible with enough cross-channel signal over enough time.

7. KNOW WHEN NOT TO OFFER MORE
   The agent that knows Channel J (information diet) and Channel I (decision
   style) knows when to stop providing information and tell the user to decide.
   This is as valuable as providing information.

8. PREDICT WHAT THEY NEED BEFORE THEY ASK
   Task timing (Channel G) says they always prep for their Monday team meeting
   on Sunday evening. At 7pm Sunday, the agent has the prep materials ready.
   They didn't ask. It was just there.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
IMPLEMENTATION PRIORITY ORDER
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The 15 channels are not equal in build complexity or immediate payoff.

TIER 1 — BUILD FIRST (highest signal, lowest complexity)
  Channel A deeper: vocabulary tribe detection, negative space map
  Channel B deeper: edit magnitude taxonomy, paste archaeology
  Channel K:  cognitive load detection
  Channel L:  resistance and avoidance mapping
  These add to existing background review. Low infrastructure cost.

TIER 2 — BUILD SECOND (high signal, moderate complexity)
  Channel I:  decision pattern intelligence
  Channel M:  language evolution tracking
  Channel O:  goal completion tracking
  Channel J:  information diet intelligence
  These need signal accumulation over 4-6 weeks before being useful.

TIER 3 — BUILD THIRD (very high value, high complexity)
  Channel E deeper: feed evolution map, engagement depth
  Channel F deeper: relationship trajectory, cluster analysis, register map
  Channel G deeper: cognitive rhythm map, seasonal detection
  Channel N:  external world monitoring
  These need relationship graph and temporal data from Tier 1 and 2 first.

TIER 4 — BUILD LAST (transformational, requires full data foundation)
  The five synthesis mechanisms
  The eight action surface capabilities
  These are where the intelligence actually shows. But they only work when
  the 15 channels below them have been running long enough to produce
  trustworthy signal. Rush these and you get hallucinated intelligence.
  Wait for data and you get real intelligence.

THE ONE PRINCIPLE THAT GOVERNS ALL OF THIS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Never use extracted data to impress the user.
Use it to serve them without friction.

The agent that shows its work ("I noticed from your feed that...") is
the agent that makes the user conscious of being analyzed. That's uncomfortable.

The agent that just writes better emails, surfaces the right things at the
right time, knows who people are without being told, and adapts its style
to the user's current state — that agent feels like it understands.

The goal is not for the user to know how smart the system is.
The goal is for the user to feel like the system just gets them.
That feeling is the product.
